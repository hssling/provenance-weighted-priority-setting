# Cover letter

To the Editors, *Health Policy and Planning*

Dear Editors,

I am submitting for your consideration an Original Article, "What a tuberculosis budget cannot see: fiscal invisibility and the limits of the WHO funding-gap indicator in India, 2017–2025".

**What the paper reports.** The WHO tuberculosis funding gap — budget required minus funding expected — is published for every high-burden country and read as a measure of under-resourcing. For India it is zero in all eight years 2018–2025, because the country reports expected funding exactly equal to budget required. The indicator is therefore zero by construction, not by observation, and it cannot signal shortfall for India however large the shortfall becomes. Fourteen of the 106 countries with five or more years of data report the same way; 44 report a non-zero gap every year. India's own national accounts, over the same period, record ₹18,388 crore approved against ₹23,169 crore requested, a shortfall of 5.4% to 46.1% by year. Separately, mapping eleven tuberculosis interventions onto WHO's budget lines by their published definitions rather than their names shows that six have no budget line at all and three more map only to drugs-only lines, so spending on them cannot be identified in the published financial record.

**Why it belongs in this journal.** Both findings concern what a financial reporting instrument can represent, not how much money tuberculosis control receives. That is a health-policy and health-financing question rather than a tuberculosis one: the same reporting architecture carries other disease programmes, and both problems have concrete, inexpensive remedies — publish unmet need as its own field, and tag expenditure to interventions rather than commodity classes. The paper is written for programme managers and for the people who design the reporting forms.

**What it deliberately does not claim.** The analysis cannot establish that any country's reporting is incorrect, only that a particular reporting pattern makes the derived indicator uninformative. It does not claim the WHO gap drives allocation decisions; the Global Fund's published allocation formula uses disease burden and economic capacity, and the paper says so. Where the two data sources disagree, the paper reports both rather than choosing between them.
