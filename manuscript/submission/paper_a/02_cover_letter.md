# Cover letter

To the Editors, *Health Policy and Planning*

Dear Editors,

I am submitting for your consideration an Original Article, "What a tuberculosis budget cannot see: fiscal invisibility and the limits of the WHO funding-gap indicator in India, 2017–2025".

**What the paper reports.** The WHO tuberculosis funding gap — budget required minus funding expected — is published for every high-burden country and read as a measure of under-resourcing. For India it is zero in all eight years 2018–2025, because the country reports expected funding exactly equal to budget required. The indicator is therefore zero by construction, not by observation, and it cannot signal shortfall for India however large the shortfall becomes. Fourteen of the 106 countries with five or more years of data report the same way; 44 report a non-zero gap every year. India's own national accounts, over the same period, record ₹18,388 crore approved against ₹23,169 crore requested, a shortfall of 5.4% to 46.1% by year. Separately, no tuberculosis intervention of eleven examined has a budget line of its own: active case finding, airborne infection control and private-sector engagement are named by WHO's data collection form inside a single line that pools fifteen activities, so what any of them costs cannot be recovered from the published record. A further finding concerns WHO's own documentation: the data dictionary distributed with the datasets abbreviates that line in a way that excludes those activities, and classifying the interventions against the dictionary rather than the form changes the number with no budget line at all from two to six.

**Why it belongs in this journal.** Both findings concern what a financial reporting instrument can represent, not how much money tuberculosis control receives, and that is a health-policy and health-financing question rather than a tuberculosis one: the same reporting architecture carries other disease programmes. The consequence is for efficiency, not only transparency. Because cost cannot be attributed to activity, cost per case found cannot be computed and no activity in the national strategic plan can be assessed for value for money, so the country with the world's largest tuberculosis burden cannot perform its own cost-effectiveness analysis of its own activities. The paper sets out four changes the national programme can make without new funding, the first of which costs nothing, and is written for programme managers and for the people who design reporting forms.

**What it deliberately does not claim.** The analysis cannot establish that any country's reporting is incorrect, only that a particular reporting pattern makes the derived indicator uninformative. It does not claim the WHO gap drives allocation decisions; the Global Fund's published allocation formula uses disease burden and economic capacity, and the paper says so. Where the two data sources disagree, the paper reports both rather than choosing between them.
