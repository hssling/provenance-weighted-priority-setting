**Subject:** Submission of an Original Article — "What a tuberculosis budget cannot see: fiscal invisibility and the limits of the WHO funding-gap indicator in India, 2017–2025"

Dear Editor,

I submit the enclosed manuscript for consideration as an Original Article.

**What the paper reports.** The paper asks two questions that the National TB Elimination Programme and the Central TB Division face directly: does the international indicator meant to signal a shortfall in TB financing register India's, and can India's TB budget say what any individual activity costs? The WHO funding gap, budget required minus funding expected, is zero for India in all eight years 2018–2025, because expected funding is reported exactly equal to budget required; it is zero by construction, not by observation. Fourteen of the 106 countries with five or more years of data report the same way, while 44 report a gap every year. India's own accounts, over the same period, record ₹18,388 crore approved against ₹23,169 crore requested, a shortfall of 5.4% to 46.1% by year. Separately, no intervention of eleven examined has a budget line of its own: active case finding, airborne infection control and private-sector engagement are named by WHO's data collection form inside a single line that pools fifteen activities. Classified against the data dictionary distributed with the data, rather than the form, five interventions would have no line instead of two.

**Why it matters for the programme.** Because cost cannot be attributed to activity, NTEP cannot compute cost per additional case found from its own accounts, and its unit costs must come from periodic facility surveys. The paper sets out four changes that need no new money — a different entry in one existing WHO field, sub-codes beneath one pooled budget line for six to eight contested activities, a reconciling footnote in the India TB Report, and a three-question check before the annual WHO return — each with the body that can make it and the evidence that would show it worked.

**What it deliberately does not claim.** The analysis cannot establish that any country's reporting is incorrect, only that one reporting pattern makes the derived indicator uninformative. It does not claim that the WHO gap drives allocation; the Global Fund's published formula uses disease burden and economic capacity, and the paper says so. Where the two sources disagree, both are reported.
